Attention is one of the few things we cannot give without also giving ourselves. That is why distraction is not only an efficiency problem. It can become a way of avoiding the difficult reality of another person, a demanding task, or our own interior life.

I feel this most clearly when I try to read. A page can be open in front of me while my mind is rehearsing a reply, checking an imagined notification, or reaching for the next useful thing. The body is present; the self is dispersed.

## Presence is not passivity

To attend is an active discipline. It is choosing, again and again, to remain with what is in front of us long enough for it to become particular.

> “Where your treasure is, there your heart will be also.” — [Matthew 6:21](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%206%3A21&version=ESV)

Jesus’ words are often read as a warning about money, but they also expose a broader pattern: our commitments train our attention. What we repeatedly reach for begins to organize our imagination.

This is why the smallest habits deserve honest scrutiny. A phone is not a moral catastrophe. But a life built from interruptions can make sustained thought, gratitude, prayer, and conversation feel strangely expensive.

## A modest practice

I am not trying to win an ascetic competition with my devices. I am trying to arrange my days so that the important things have a real chance to be heard.

1. Begin the day with a few unhurried minutes of Scripture and silence before opening a feed.
2. Keep one block of work for a single difficult problem; turn off the invitations to switch context.
3. Read physical books often enough that slowness stops feeling like a defect.
4. When someone is speaking, let the phone remain out of reach.

None of this is dramatic. That is encouraging. Faithfulness is usually made of ordinary returns: returning to the page, the task, the person, the prayer.

## The attention of a maker

This matters in engineering too. Software rewards fast feedback, but not every valuable question yields to a quick experiment. Sometimes the work is to stay with a confused requirement, to listen carefully to the person reporting a bug, or to notice that a neat abstraction has hidden the very distinction the product needs.

The goal is not intensity for its own sake. It is availability: to God, to neighbors, and to the work we have been given. Attention is one of the names love takes when it becomes concrete.
