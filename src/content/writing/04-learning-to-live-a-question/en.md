I like answers. They make a map of the world possible. In engineering, a correct model can turn a day of debugging into a small, satisfying change. In theology and philosophy, however, some questions do not become less important because they resist immediate closure.

Learning to live a question is not a performance of uncertainty. It is the refusal to mistake a premature answer for wisdom.

## Conviction and curiosity belong together

Christian faith makes claims about reality. It is not a vague preference for spiritual atmosphere. Yet confidence in what is revealed need not produce contempt for what remains difficult, partial, or mysterious.

| A false choice | A better posture |
| :-- | :-- |
| Certainty *or* curiosity | Conviction that keeps listening |
| Questions as threats | Questions as invitations to seek truth faithfully |
| Information as possession | Understanding as a responsibility |

The difference matters. A person who only collects conclusions can become brittle. A person who only collects questions can become unmoored. The task is to become the kind of person who can receive an answer with gratitude and hold an unresolved question without panic.

## Study as formation

I have found that serious study changes more than what I know. It exposes the impatience behind my opinions, teaches me to distinguish confidence from volume, and asks whether I am seeking truth or merely material for winning.

That applies to technical work as much as biblical study. A strong engineer needs more than a preferred framework. He needs the habit of asking what the system is actually doing, what trade-off is being made, and who bears its cost.

Here is a question I return to when learning something new:

> Am I trying to master this subject, or am I allowing it to correct my assumptions?

The first impulse is useful; it gives us tools. The second makes us teachable. We need both.

## A patient way forward

When a question stays open, I try a simple sequence:

- state what I know and why I think I know it;
- name the point of tension precisely, without making it theatrical;
- read people who have earned the right to disagree with me;
- bring the question into prayer, conversation, and time;
- act faithfully on what is clear while I wait for greater clarity.

This is slow work. But some of the most important things in life—wisdom, character, communion with God, love of neighbor—cannot be optimized on a dashboard. They are received and practiced over a lifetime.
